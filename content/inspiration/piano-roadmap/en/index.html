Learning path · adult beginner

Piano Chord Roadmap

Twelve stages from “where is C?” to reading lead sheets with 7th and 9th chords. Each stage tells you what to understand, practise, memorise and ignore, and how it leads to the next. You play songs from Stage 3 onwards.

The 12 stages

01

Phase A · Foundations

The keyboard and the 12 notes

Find any note on the keyboard without thinking.

CC♯
D♭		DD♯
E♭		EF F♯
G♭		GG♯
A♭		AA♯
B♭		B

The 12 notes. A black key has two names: ♯ (sharp) = one key up, ♭ (flat) = one key down.

1Understand

  • Seven letter names (A–G) repeat across the whole keyboard.
  • Black keys come in groups of 2 and 3. They are your map.
  • Sharp (♯) = one key to the right. Flat (♭) = one key to the left.
  • A half step is the very next key, black or white. A whole step is two half steps. This is the measuring tape for everything that follows.

2Practise on the piano

  • Play every C on the piano. Then every F. Then every G.
  • Name random white keys out loud. Aim for under 2 seconds each.
  • Play all 12 keys from C to C and count the half steps.
  • Find each black key by both names (C♯ = D♭).

3Memorise

  • Where C is (left of the two black keys).
  • The 12 note names in order, including both names of each black key.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Reading notes on the staff (treble and bass clef).
  • Rhythm notation and time signatures.
  • Octave numbers (C4, C5) and rare names like E♯ or C♭.

5Connects to the next stage

Chords are built by counting half steps. Once you can count them, you can build any chord.

Ready to move on when

You can find any of the 12 notes instantly and count half steps between two keys.

02

Phase A · Foundations

Major and minor chords

Build any major or minor triad by counting.

Learn them in shape families
Shape (bottom – middle – top)MajorMinor
1All whiteC F GDm Em Am
2White – black – whiteD E ACm Fm Gm
3Black – white – blackD♭ E♭ A♭C♯m F♯m G♯m
4The odd onesF♯ B B♭E♭m B♭m Bm

Chords with the same black/white shape feel the same under your hand. Learn one family at a time, row by row.

1Understand

  • A triad is three notes: root, 3rd, 5th.
  • Major = root + 4 half steps + 3 half steps. Sounds bright.
  • Minor = root + 3 half steps + 4 half steps. Sounds darker.
  • Chord symbols: a letter alone is major (C). A small m is minor (Cm).

2Practise on the piano

  • Family 1 first: C, F, G, Dm, Em, Am (all white keys).
  • Play each major chord, then lower the middle note: C → Cm, F → Fm.
  • Right hand fingers 1–3–5. Left hand fingers 5–3–1.
  • Add one shape family per week until you have all 24 chords.

3Memorise

  • The two recipes: 4 + 3 (major) and 3 + 4 (minor).
  • Family 1 by feel: C, F, G, Am, Dm, Em.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Correct spelling (E♯ vs F). For now, find the right keys.
  • 7th chords, sus chords and other suffixes.
  • Diminished and augmented chords (they come in Stage 6 and 10).

5Connects to the next stage

With C, F, G and Am you can already play a huge number of songs. Stage 3 puts them to work.

Ready to move on when

You can play all 12 major and 12 minor triads, slowly but correctly.

Phase B · Playing with chords

Your first progressions and songs

Accompany a simple song from chord symbols.

C1 2 3 4
F1 2 3 4
G1 2 3 4
C1 2 3 4

Chord symbols sit above the lyrics or melody. Play the chord where it appears. Hold it until the next symbol.

PatternBeat 1234
A · BlockLH root + RH chordholdholdhold
B · PulseLH root + RH chordRH chordRH chordRH chord
C · Bass + chordLH rootRH chordLH rootRH chord

Three accompaniment patterns, easiest first. LH = left hand, RH = right hand.

1Understand

  • A lead sheet shows lyrics or melody with chord symbols above.
  • You play the chord where the symbol appears and hold it until the next one.
  • Most songs use a small family of chords. In C these are C, Dm, Em, F, G and Am.
  • Steady rhythm matters more than the right voicing.

2Practise on the piano

  • Loop C – F – G – C, four beats per chord, with a metronome.
  • Loop C – G – Am – F the same way.
  • Left hand: the root note only. Right hand: the triad.
  • Pick one easy song in C or G from a chord-symbol songbook and play it with pattern A, then B, then C.

3Memorise

  • The six chords of C major: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am.
  • The three accompaniment patterns in the table.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Why these chords belong together (Stage 6 explains it).
  • Playing the melody at the same time.
  • Complex rhythms and syncopation.

5Connects to the next stage

You will notice your right hand jumps between chords. Inversions fix that.

Ready to move on when

You can play one song start to finish in steady time without stopping.

04

Phase B · Playing with chords

Inversions

Change chords with minimal hand movement.

Why it matters: C – F – G
Jumpy
Smooth

Same three chords. Smooth version: keep shared notes, move the others to the nearest key. Your hand barely moves.

1Understand

  • An inversion uses the same notes in a different order.
  • Root position: root at the bottom. 1st inversion: 3rd at the bottom. 2nd inversion: 5th at the bottom.
  • The chord name does not change. It is still C.
  • A slash chord like C/E means: C chord, E in the bass.

2Practise on the piano

  • Play C in all three positions, up and down the keyboard.
  • Do the same for F, G and Am.
  • Play C – F/C – G/B – C (the smooth row) until it feels automatic.
  • Go back to your Stage 3 song and use the nearest inversion for each chord.

3Memorise

  • The rule: keep shared notes, move the others to the nearest key.
  • The smooth voicings of C – F – G – Am in C major.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Classical inversion labels (6, 6/4).
  • 3rd inversions (they need four notes, see Stage 11).

5Connects to the next stage

Now you can play smoothly. Next you learn why C, F, G and Am belong together: the major scale.

Ready to move on when

You can loop C – G – Am – F with your right hand staying in one place.

05

Phase C · Understanding keys

The major scale and scale degrees

Understand what a key is.

1Understand

  • Every major scale uses the same step pattern: W W H W W W H.
  • A key is the scale a song is built on. The first note is home (the tonic).
  • Each scale note gets a number from 1 to 7. These scale degrees work in every key.
  • Starting the pattern on G forces F♯. That is why G major has one sharp.

2Practise on the piano

  • C major and G major, hands separately, one octave.
  • Fingering (C, G, D): RH 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5, LH 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1.
  • F major: RH 1-2-3-4-1-2-3-4 (thumb goes under after B♭).
  • Say the numbers while you play. Then jump: “play 4”, “play 5”, “play 1”.

3Memorise

  • The pattern W W H W W W H.
  • Scales of C, G, D and F.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Modes (Dorian, Lydian …).
  • Harmonic and melodic minor scales.
  • Playing scales fast or hands together.

5Connects to the next stage

Build a triad on each of the 7 scale notes and you get the chords of the key.

Ready to move on when

You can build the major scale from any note using the step pattern.

Phase C · Understanding keys

Diatonic chords and Roman numerals

Know which chords belong to a key and name them by number.

Top (5th)GABCDEF
Middle (3rd)EFGABCD
RootCDEFGAB
ChordCDmEmFGAmBdim
NumeralIiiiiiIVVvivii°

Take every other scale note, starting on each degree. Only white keys in C. UPPERCASE = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished.

KeyIiiiiiIVVvivii°
C majorCDmEmFGAmBdim
G majorGAmBmCDEmF♯dim
F majorFGmAmB♭CDmEdim
D majorDEmF♯mGABmC♯dim

The pattern is the same in every key: major minor minor major major minor diminished. All 12 keys come in Stage 8.

1Understand

  • Stack every other note of the scale and you get 7 chords. These are the diatonic chords of the key.
  • Roman numerals name each chord by its position: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°.
  • Uppercase = major. Lowercase = minor. ° = diminished.
  • Numbers let you think in any key: “I – IV – V” is C–F–G in C, and G–C–D in G.

2Practise on the piano

  • Play the 7 chords up the scale in C, G, F and D. Say the numeral aloud.
  • Take your Stage 3 song and write the numerals under the chords.
  • Call out a random numeral in a key and play it (“G major, vi” → Em).

3Memorise

  • The pattern: I ii iii IV V vi vii°.
  • I, IV and V are the three major chords. vi is the minor one you will meet most.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • How to use vii° (rare in pop music).
  • Chords from outside the key (borrowed chords, secondary dominants).

5Connects to the next stage

A chord progression is just a pattern of numerals. Stage 7 gives you the most common ones.

Ready to move on when

You can name the 7 chords of C, G, F and D without looking.

Phase C · Understanding keys

Common chord progressions

Recognise the patterns that most songs are made of.

Progressionin Cin Gin Fin D
I – IV – V – IThe basic family. Folk, rock ’n’ roll, children’s songs.C – F – G – CG – C – D – GF – B♭ – C – FD – G – A – D
I – V – vi – IVThe classic pop loop.C – G – Am – FG – D – Em – CF – C – Dm – B♭D – A – Bm – G
vi – IV – I – VSame four chords, starting on the minor. Sounds sadder.Am – F – C – GEm – C – G – DDm – B♭ – F – CBm – G – D – A

1Understand

  • A handful of numeral patterns turn up in a very large number of songs.
  • Once you know the pattern, you can play it in any key you know.
  • The V chord pulls back to I. Listen for that “coming home” feeling.

2Practise on the piano

  • Each progression in C, then G, then F. Use smooth inversions from Stage 4.
  • Accompaniment pattern B or C, with a metronome.
  • Listen to songs you like and try to hear when the music returns to I.
  • Look up chord charts of songs you know and find these patterns in them.

3Memorise

  • The five progressions as numerals, not as chord names.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Jazz substitutions.
  • Key changes within a song.
  • Long chord charts with many unusual chords.

5Connects to the next stage

To play these patterns in every key you need all 12 keys. The Circle of Fifths organises them.

Ready to move on when

You can play I – V – vi – IV in C, G and F from memory.

08

Phase C · Understanding keys

The Circle of Fifths and all 12 keys

Organise all keys on one map.

Order of sharpsF♯C♯G♯D♯A♯E♯B♯“Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle”
Order of flatsB♭E♭A♭D♭G♭C♭F♭The same letters backwards (“BEAD” + G C F)

Shortcut sharps: last sharp + one half step up = key name (F♯, C♯ → D major). Shortcut flats: second-to-last flat = key name (B♭, E♭, A♭ → E♭ major). F major (1 flat) you just memorise.

1Understand

  • Going clockwise, each key is a 5th higher and adds one sharp.
  • Going counter-clockwise, each key adds one flat.
  • Neighbours are closely related. The key’s I, IV and V sit side by side.
  • The inner ring shows the relative minor of each key. It shares the same key signature.
  • To transpose a song, move every chord the same number of steps around the circle.

2Practise on the piano

  • Add new keys in circle order: C → G and F → D and B♭ → A and E♭ → E and A♭ → the rest.
  • For each new key: scale, the 7 diatonic chords, and I – IV – V – I with inversions.
  • Draw the circle from memory once a week.
  • Transpose your favourite progression into three keys.

3Memorise

  • The circle order: C G D A E B F♯ / D♭ A♭ E♭ B♭ F.
  • How many sharps or flats each key has.
  • The order of sharps (F C G D A E B) and flats (B E A D G C F).

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Keys with 7 sharps or flats (C♯, C♭). Use D♭ and B instead.
  • Writing key signatures on a staff.

5Connects to the next stage

The inner ring is the door to minor keys.

Ready to move on when

Given any key, you can name its sharps/flats and its I, IV, V.

Phase C · Understanding keys

Minor keys

Play and recognise songs in minor.

Keyiii°IIIivv / VVIVII
A minorAmBdimCDmEm / EFG
E minorEmF♯dimGAmBm / BCD
D minorDmEdimFGmAm / AB♭C

A minor uses the same chords as C major, starting from Am. Songs in minor often swap the minor v for a major V (E instead of Em in A minor). That gives a stronger pull home.

1Understand

  • Every major key has a relative minor. It starts on degree 6 (vi).
  • Relative keys share all notes and chords. Only “home” is different.
  • In a minor key the numerals start from the minor chord: i ii° III iv v VI VII.
  • A song with no sharps or flats that feels sad and ends on Am is in A minor, not C major.

2Practise on the piano

  • In A minor: Am – Dm – E – Am (i – iv – V – i).
  • In A minor: Am – F – C – G (i – VI – III – VII).
  • Repeat in E minor and D minor.

3Memorise

  • Relative pairs: C/Am, G/Em, F/Dm, D/Bm, B♭/Gm.
  • In minor, the V is often major.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Harmonic and melodic minor scales in detail.
  • Modes.

5Connects to the next stage

You now know the chord families of all keys. Next you add colour to the chords.

Ready to move on when

You can tell whether a simple song is in a major key or its relative minor.

Each suffix changes or adds one note. The letter before the suffix is always the root.

1Understand

  • A suffix after the letter changes or adds one note. The letter is still the root.
  • sus chords replace the 3rd. They sound open and unresolved.
  • add9 and 6 add colour to a normal triad.
  • The note after a slash is the bass note (your left hand).

2Practise on the piano

  • Csus4 → C, Gsus4 → G. Hear the tension resolve.
  • Replace some chords in a progression with add9 versions (Cadd9, Fadd9).
  • Play slash chords from a chart: left hand the bass note, right hand the chord.

3Memorise

  • What sus2, sus4, add9, 6 and a slash do.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • 9, 11 and 13 chords (Stage 12).
  • Rare symbols like 7♯9 or 7♭13.

5Connects to the next stage

The most important added note is the 7th. It gets its own stage.

Ready to move on when

You can read a chart with sus, add9 and slash chords without stopping.

11

Phase D · Richer harmony

Seventh chords

Understand the five main 7th chords.

“7” alonemeans the flat 7th (B♭ in C). This is the dominant 7th.
“maj”changes only the 7th: the natural 7th (B in C).
“m”changes only the 3rd: the minor 3rd (E♭ in C).

The 7th chords of C major: Cmaj7 Dm7 Em7 Fmaj7 G7 Am7 Bm7♭5. The V7 (G7) is the only “7” chord in the key.

1Understand

  • A 7th chord is a triad plus one more note: the 7th.
  • Three rules explain most symbols: “7” = flat 7th, “maj” changes the 7th, “m” changes the 3rd.
  • V7 has a strong pull back to I (G7 → C).
  • ii7 – V7 – Imaj7 is the core of jazz harmony (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7).

2Practise on the piano

  • On one root, play C → Cmaj7 → C7 → Cm7. Hear each change.
  • ii7 – V7 – Imaj7 in C, F and B♭.
  • Four notes means four positions. Practise the 3rd inversion (7th at the bottom).
  • Simple voicing: LH root, RH the other three notes.

3Memorise

  • The formulas of maj7, 7, m7, m7♭5 and dim7.
  • The 7th-chord pattern of a major key: Imaj7 ii7 iii7 IVmaj7 V7 vi7 viiø7.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • mMaj7 and aug7 (rare; look them up when they appear).
  • Altered dominants (7♭9, 7♯9).

5Connects to the next stage

Extended chords are 7th chords with even more notes stacked on top.

Ready to move on when

You can play Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 smoothly and name each note.

Phase D · Richer harmony

Extended chords and voicings

Play 9, 11 and 13 chords the way pianists really do.

NumberSame note asChordFull formulaWhat piano players usually leave out
92C91 3 5 ♭7 9the 5th
114C111 3 5 ♭7 9 11the 3rd (it clashes with the 11)
136C131 3 5 ♭7 9 11 13the 5th, 9th and 11th

1Understand

  • 9, 11 and 13 continue the stack of 3rds above the 7th.
  • 9 = the 2nd, 11 = the 4th, 13 = the 6th, just one octave higher.
  • A number above 7 implies the 7th is also there (C9 includes B♭).
  • You rarely play every note. Pianists leave out the 5th and other notes that clash.

2Practise on the piano

  • C9, Cmaj9 and C13 with the voicings shown above.
  • Dm9 – G13 – Cmaj9 as a richer ii – V – I.
  • Take a song you know and replace plain chords with 7ths and 9ths.

3Memorise

  • 9 = 2, 11 = 4, 13 = 6.
  • What to leave out: the 5th almost always.

4Safe to ignore for now

  • Upper-structure triads and polychords.
  • Full theory of chord tensions. Learn by ear and by chart.

5Connects to the next stage

From here, most learning happens through songs. Every new chart teaches you a new chord.

Ready to move on when

You can read a lead sheet with 7th and 9th chords and play it in time.

The song thread

Songs are not the final stage. They run alongside everything from Stage 3 on.

Stages 3–4Songs with 3–4 chords in C or G. Block chords, then simple patterns.
Stages 5–7Write numerals under every chart. Play the same song in two keys.
Stages 8–9Transpose songs to a comfortable singing key. Add songs in minor.
Stages 10–12Charts with sus, add9 and 7th chords. Try broken chords and arpeggios.
