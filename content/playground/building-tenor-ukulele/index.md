## Motivation

I once wanted to learn violin making. I had the application papers and never sent them.
Then I met Alexandra and Pascal, two professional violin makers. I do not need another violin right now, so I asked: why not build a ukulele instead? It takes less time than a violin, and it is a proper way into the craft and into the world of instrument making.
The build itself is traditional throughout: hand planes, scrapers, fretsaw and wooden bars for gluing. The one exception is the decoration. For that I used a CO₂ laser cutter, and I show exactly where below.

## The build

This is a side project. Alexandra and Pascal are violin makers. I asked whether I could build a tenor ukulele under their guidance, and they said yes. Their workshop is in Lichtenhain an der Bergbahn, in the Thuringian Forest. Whenever I have time, I drive there and carry on where I left off.

What follows is a selection of steps, not the full process. More details will come as I go.

### First steps: plane, plane, and scrape

You start with a lot of wood. It has to be sawn to size, planed to thickness, glued up, and then planed and scraped. The scraping is done with cabinet scrapers.

The top is hazel spruce (*Haselfichte*). This rare wood is chosen for its excellent resonance; it carries vibrations very well. For the back I wanted something reddish, and we settled on bloodwood. All the wood comes from sustainable, verifiably legal forestry. Black veneer is used as well. The fingerboard will be Indian rosewood, which has a dark colour that leans towards violet-brown.

### Top

#### Sound hole reinforcement

The sound hole is stabilised with veneer. I used two different colours for a cleaner look. First a recess is cut, then the veneer is glued in, and finally the excess is planed off flush.

![Sound hole reinforcement](Lochstabilisator.jpg)
![Sound hole](schalloch.jpg)

#### Decorating the top

I wanted something on the top that was mine. I decided on an ink drawing.

First I made an SVG and lasered it onto the top with the CO₂ laser. Then I traced the lines in ink.

![The laser cutter](laser-machine.jpg)
![Lasering the design](lasern.jpg)
![Tracing in ink](painting.jpg)

#### Cutting the sound hole and the outline

Then the sound hole is cut out, and the outline of the top is cut with a fretsaw.

![Sawing the sound hole](Schalloch-saegen.jpg)
![The top](Decke.jpg)

#### Reinforcing the sound hole

The sound hole is a fragile spot, so it is reinforced from the underside of the top. The reinforcement then has to be bevelled towards the hole so that it is invisible when you look in from the outside.

![Nothing of the reinforcement should show when you look in from the outside](Schallochverstärkung.jpg)

#### Bracing the top

The top is braced as well, above all around the bridge, where the strings pull.

![Bracing the top](decken-verstaerkung.jpg)
![Shaping the braces with a mini plane](deckenverstaerkung-miniHobel.jpg)

### Neck

You start with a rectangular block that is glued up, and then you remove wood. And more wood. You can watch the pile of shavings grow.

To give the headstock a bit of elegance, I worked black veneer into it here as well (see the photo of the head, top view).

### Back

I worked on the back in parallel with the top. It is braced too, of course. The braces start out rectangular and are then shaped so there are no hard edges and the plate can vibrate more freely.

## Methods

For some of the glue-ups I used the Spanish method instead of clamps. It is traditional in guitar making. It is cheap and minimal: to glue two pieces of wood together, they are pressed against each other with beech bars. The bars are rectangular so they do not slip the way a round dowel would.
